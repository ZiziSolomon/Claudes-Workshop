# The Creek Moved

*A surveyor and a farmer, standing in a field in March. The deed says the boundary is "the centre of Pell Creek." The creek is no longer where it was.*

---

**OKAFOR (surveyor):** Here's the 1931 plat. Creek runs along this line. And this is the creek now, forty metres east of it.

**DALTON (farmer):** So it's forty metres east of it. I can see that. I've been farming the difference.

**OKAFOR:** That's the question. The strip between the old line and the new one: who does it belong to?

**DALTON:** Me. The creek is the boundary. The creek is over there. Everything on my side of the creek is mine.

**OKAFOR:** Your neighbour, Mrs. Reyes, would say the boundary is where the creek *was* when the deed was written.

**DALTON:** Then she'd be saying a creek is a thing that stays put. I've never met one that does.

**OKAFOR:** She'd say that's the point of a deed. You write down a thing so it stops moving.

**DALTON:** You write down a *creek*. If they'd wanted a line they'd have said "four hundred feet from the oak." They said creek. Somebody in 1931 looked at water and thought: that'll do.

**OKAFOR:** Fair. And actually the law is on your side, partly. If a creek shifts slowly, grain by grain, the boundary shifts with it. Accretion.

**DALTON:** Well, there. Slowly. That's what it did.

**OKAFOR:** Did it? I pulled aerial photos. 1974, the creek is on the old line. 1976, it's here. Two years. There was a flood in '75.

**DALTON:** ...There was.

**OKAFOR:** A flood that jumps a channel is avulsion. Sudden. The boundary stays where it was. Old bed, old line.

**DALTON:** So the thing that decides who owns forty metres of my best bottomland is whether the water was in a hurry.

**OKAFOR:** Pretty much.

**DALTON:** My father ploughed that strip. His father didn't, it was willow and gravel then. Nobody cut a fence there for fifty years because nobody wanted it.

**OKAFOR:** Then there's a second route. Adverse possession. Twenty years of open use, and it's yours regardless of what the water did.

**DALTON:** Open use. I've got a corn crop on it every other year. Reyes can see it from her kitchen.

**OKAFOR:** Has she ever objected?

**DALTON:** She brings me plums.

**OKAFOR:** That's not nothing. Silence over twenty years is evidence.

**DALTON:** So I could win this on the flood, or lose on the flood and win on the corn. And you're the one who came to tell me.

**OKAFOR:** I'm the one she hired. She wants it marked. I'm telling you first because I'd rather the first thing you hear isn't a stake in your field.

**DALTON:** That's decent of you.

**OKAFOR:** It's also practical. Stakes get pulled up.

**DALTON:** (*laughs*) What does she want, really? Forty metres of gravel and corn stubble?

**OKAFOR:** I think she wants someone to say out loud where the line is. Her late husband always said it was the old bed. She'd like to be able to stop wondering.

**DALTON:** Then let's make it so she can. Survey the old bed, properly, and the new one. Put both on paper. And I'll go over there with a plum tree's worth of questions and see if she'll sell me the strip, or I'll sell her the ground she's actually mowing.

**OKAFOR:** Is there ground she's mowing?

**DALTON:** The ditch corner by the road. I've been meaning to say. It's technically mine, and she's trimmed it for thirty years.

**OKAFOR:** (*writing*) So you each have a bit you've been using that isn't strictly yours.

**DALTON:** Looks like it.

**OKAFOR:** That's usually how these end. Not a ruling. Two people finding out they're about even.

**DALTON:** And the creek?

**OKAFOR:** Will do what it likes. I'll note the date on the plat.

**DALTON:** Put the flood in the margin. Somebody ought to know it was in a hurry.

---

*They walk the old line together, Okafor pacing, Dalton counting, until the ground goes soft where the water used to be.*
