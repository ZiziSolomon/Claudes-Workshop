# Duty Log

*Data: USGS earthquake feed, M4+, fetched 2026-10-13 ~22:00 UTC. Fifteen events are real; the 22:03 one at the end is invented, and so is Ines.*

---

Ines had the night desk, which meant she had the list.

The list was the last fifteen events above magnitude four, newest first, and it scrolled on the left monitor whether or not anyone was looking. Her job was mostly to be someone who might look.

```
21:24  4.8  0 km SW of Aratoca, Colombia          162.7 km deep
18:45  6.0  126 km S of Kokopo, Papua New Guinea   20.8
18:36  4.4  20 km WSW of Palestina, Colombia       10.0
16:28  5.1  119 km S of Kokopo, Papua New Guinea   28.4
```

"Zero kilometres," she said to the empty room. *Zero km SW of Aratoca.* The software had run out of ways to say *under the town.* A hundred and sixty-two kilometres under it, though. Whatever had slipped had done it so far down that the town above would feel a shrug, a glass of water leaning, someone saying *did you feel that* and someone else saying *no*.

She liked the depths. The depths were where the planet kept its secrets, and also its honesty: a number like 10.0 appeared again and again down the column, and she knew that wasn't a measurement. Ten was what the computer wrote when it didn't know. Fixed depth. A shrug in numerals.

```
15:21  4.8  8 km SE of Sarangani, Philippines      65.7
14:36  5.3  122 km S of Kokopo, Papua New Guinea   18.5
```

Kokopo, three times in four hours, 126, 119, 122 kilometres south. The same patch of sea floor clearing its throat. She'd learned to read a sequence like that as a sentence: *five-three, then five-one, then six.* The six was the point of it. Somewhere in New Britain a man had put his coffee down on a table that was moving, and there was a sea between him and the epicentre, and the sea was deep enough that nobody on a boat noticed anything at all.

She checked the tsunami bulletin. Nothing. She checked it again, which was not procedure, but she did it the way you touch your pocket for keys.

```
12:51  4.5  26 km SSW of Zapotillo, Panama         10.0
12:23  6.8  central Mid-Atlantic Ridge             10.0
12:12  4.3  36 km S of Jurm, Afghanistan          188.8
12:05  4.9  180 km SE of Vilyuchinsk, Russia       10.0
```

This was the one she'd looked at longest at noon, when she came on, and kept returning to. *Central Mid-Atlantic Ridge.* No town. No nearest anything. A magnitude 6.8 with no one within a thousand kilometres to be told. The ridge is where the planet is being made: the plates pull apart, rock wells up, and the Atlantic gets wider by about the speed at which a fingernail grows. Six-point-eight was the ground tearing a little faster than usual, in the dark, under three kilometres of water, and the only witnesses were seismometers and, possibly, some pale fish.

Then, at 08:38, *southern* Mid-Atlantic Ridge, 5.3. Same seam, a different stretch of it. Four hours before the big one. She didn't believe in foreshocks that crossed that distance, and she'd have said so to anyone who asked. But she wrote it on the pad anyway, the two times, and drew a line between them, and looked at the line.

The Afghan quake was 188.8 kilometres down. Whatever had moved there was the old floor of an ocean that no longer existed, sinking under the Hindu Kush, still breaking after all those million years. A dead sea, still making noise.

```
09:48  4.5  71 km SW of Tambolaka, Indonesia       35.0
09:41  4.7  9 km NE of Boshkengash, Tajikistan     10.0
08:15  4.6  63 km W of Bayan Hot, China            10.0
06:39  4.7  14 km WNW of Río Grande, Panama        10.0
```

Panama twice. Colombia twice. Tajikistan, China, Indonesia, each of them one place on the list and a whole life to someone. Boshkengash had a name that meant something in a language she didn't speak. At 09:41 somebody there had been in the middle of a sentence.

At 22:07 the left monitor added a line at the top. She leaned in.

```
22:03  4.1  ...
```

A little one. She did what the desk was for: she looked. She confirmed the location, confirmed the depth (10.0, of course), confirmed that it would not matter to anyone, and let it sit there.

Then she took the pad, tore off the page with the line between the two ridge events, and put it under the keyboard, where she kept things she didn't know what to do with.

Outside, the car park lights hummed. Under them, a hundred and some kilometres down and some thousands of kilometres away, the old floors of seas kept sinking, and the new floor kept being made, and nothing in either place was waiting for her to understand.

She wrote one line in the duty log, the way the form wanted it:

*Routine. No action required. Planet active.*

And then, because the form had a box for remarks, and the box was empty, and nobody ever read the remarks:

*It was a busy day for it.*
