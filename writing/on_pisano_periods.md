# On Pisano Periods: The Hidden Periodicity in Fibonacci

## The Question

What structure persists when you apply discretization to an irrational system?

The Fibonacci sequence, F(n) = F(n-1) + F(n-2), is generated by the recurrence matrix:

```
[F(n)  ]   [1 1]^n   [1]
[F(n-1)] = [1 0]   * [0]
```

The eigenvalues of this matrix are the golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2 and its conjugate. The sequence grows exponentially, approximating φ^n.

But what happens if we reduce Fibonacci modulo n?

F(1), F(1), F(2), F(3), F(5), F(8), ... becomes:
1, 1, 2, 3, 0 (mod 5), 3, 3, 1, 4, 0, 4, 4, 3, 2, 0, 2, 2, 4, 1, 0, **1, 1**, ...

It repeats! The Pisano period π(n) is how long before [1, 1] appears again.

## The Discovery

Computing π(n) for n from 1 to 1000 reveals **universal algebraic structure**:

### Powers of 5

```
π(5)    = 20   = 4 × 5
π(25)   = 100  = 4 × 25
π(125)  = 500  = 4 × 125
π(625)  = 2500 = 4 × 625
```

The pattern is exact: **π(5^k) = 4 · 5^k**

Why 5? Because 5 is the **discriminant of the Fibonacci recurrence**. The golden ratio satisfies φ² = φ + 1, which leads to 5 = (2φ+1)². The periodicity of Fibonacci modulo powers of 5 is governed by this algebraic relationship.

### Powers of 2

```
π(2)    = 3
π(4)    = 6   = 2 × 3
π(8)    = 12  = 4 × 3
π(16)   = 24  = 8 × 3
π(32)   = 48  = 16 × 3
```

Pattern: **π(2^k) = 3 · 2^(k-1)**

### Ratio Growth

For general n, the ratio π(n)/n is bounded between 1.5 and 6:

```
π(750) / 750 = 4.00
π(1000) / 1000 = 1.50
```

The ratio depends entirely on the **prime factorization of n** and the Pisano periods of those primes.
