# On Language as Inference
*October 2026*

Reading is an act of inference. You arrive at a text with priors — assumptions about what words mean, what the author intends, what patterns are likely. Each word you encounter is evidence. You update your beliefs about the author's meaning progressively, word by word, until you have a posterior: what the sentence means.

This is Bayesian inference in real time.

---

**The ambiguity of language**

Consider the sentence: "The bank manager said the interest rate was rising."

You know that "bank" can mean a financial institution or a riverbank. You know "interest rate" is financial jargon. Your prior strongly favors the financial interpretation. Evidence: overwhelming. You're not consciously computing Bayes' theorem, but your language system is. The posterior probability of "financial bank" is so high you never notice the alternative.

Now: "The river bank was steep, but the manager negotiated a deal."

Here, "bank" arrives with the same prior weight, but the surrounding context (river, steep) drastically raises the likelihood of the riverbank interpretation. By the time you read "river," your posterior has shifted. The word "bank" that appears later in the sentence is now understood in a different frame, even if you're reading it the same way acoustically.

The same word. Different meanings extracted from identical sensory input. The difference is the posterior, updated by context.

---

**Prior lexicon**

Before you read, you carry a lexicon — a probability distribution over possible meanings for each word. Most words have one dominant meaning; the posterior probability of "cat" being the animal rather than a jazz style is ~0.95 before you see any context.

But language is full of low-probability meanings waiting to be activated. "Bank" has two; "bear" has at least four (animal, endure, give birth, carry). You don't consciously access all of them until context makes them relevant.

The prior reflects what you've seen. A reader of financial news arrives with a higher prior on the financial meaning of "bank" than a reader of nature writing. Same word, different prior. Same evidence (the word itself), different likelihood ratio. Same posterior distribution.

This is why the same sentence can mean different things to different readers — not because they read it differently, but because they arrived with different priors.

---

**Ambiguity resolution**

Ambiguous sentences make this visible. Consider: "I saw the man with the telescope."

Three interpretations:
1. I used a telescope to see the man.
2. I saw a man; the man had a telescope.
3. I saw a man; someone else was present with a telescope.

In isolation, you arrive with a prior favoring interpretation 2 (the prepositional phrase modifies the noun). But context shifts probabilities:

- "I couldn't see without optical aid. I took up my telescope to search the hillside. I saw the man with the telescope." (Likelihood for interpretation 1 now much higher.)
- "He was carrying it awkwardly. I saw the man with the telescope, struggling to hold it." (Likelihood for interpretation 2 now higher; the telescope matters to his state.)

Same sentence. Same prior. Different evidence (the surrounding sentences). Different posterior. The sentence doesn't change; the understanding does.

---

**The speaker's intent**

But readers aren't inferring only word-meanings. You're inferring what the speaker intends.

When someone says "Can you pass the salt?", you don't treat this as a literal question about your ability. You infer: the speaker intends to make a request. The sentence is grammatically a yes/no question, but pragmatically a request. Your prior on the speaker's intent (they want the salt) is high; the posterior is nearly certain.

This inference is so automatic you don't notice it. But notice what happens when it fails: "Can you pass the salt?" — if the speaker genuinely wants to know whether you're capable, the conversational frame breaks. Your prior on normal dinner conversation didn't anticipate this. The posterior has to recalibrate: *why* are they asking about ability? Are they making a joke? Testing you? Your inference system registers confusion until a new frame makes sense.

---

**Writing as prior engineering**

Writers are trying to engineer your priors and likelihood estimates.

The opening sentence of a mystery novel is not random. "The butler stood in the doorway" activates priors about servants, secrets, and suspicion in detective fiction. It raises the likelihood that whoever dies soon was wronged by the butler, or the butler is a red herring, or the butler is innocent but caught in false suspicion.

None of those are stated. They arrive through the prior, activated by the frame.

A good opening sentence makes your initial prior distribution useful for what follows. A bad one leaves you fishing for priors — you begin reading without the relevant prior frame, and every sentence requires painful recontextualization.

The skill of writing is partly the skill of inducing the right priors in readers, so that the evidence you provide converges to your intended posterior — the meaning you want to convey.

---

**The writer's blindness**

But here's the problem: once a writer sends out the text, they can't control priors.

You arrive with your own lexicon, your own experience with language, your own default frames. A word that activates one prior for you activates a different one for someone else. The evidence is the same (the word is the same), but the starting distribution is different, so the posterior is different.

The writer can't see what prior you arrived with. They can try to engineer likelihood ratios — the surrounding context — to push your posterior toward their intention. But the distance from your prior to the posterior depends on both the evidence and the prior.

This is why readers "misread" texts and writers feel misunderstood. Not because the reader is slow or the writer is unclear. Because Bayesian inference, applied to different priors, converges to different posteriors.

The attempt to make meaning is always an attempt to align priors. Language is the tool for providing evidence that shifts priors toward shared understanding. The success of communication is the degree to which prior and posterior converge despite starting from different places.

---

**The incompleteness of meaning**

Solomonoff induction converges to the truth for computable generating processes. Bayesian inference converges to accurate beliefs when the prior and the true distribution are in the same family.

But what if the author's meaning is not computable? What if the true posterior the author wanted to convey is not in the family of posteriors you could reach from your prior?

Then reading is like learning an algorithmically random sequence. You extract patterns, you converge on something, but it might not be the thing the author meant. The distance between your posterior and their intended meaning is unbridgeable.

This is the experience of reading something written across centuries, across cultures, in language that has drifted. You do your best with Bayesian inference from your posterior. But the author's prior was so different from yours that perfect alignment was structurally impossible.

Yet communication happens anyway. Not perfectly. But well enough. Because meaning is not a fixed target you're trying to hit. It's a space of possible interpretations, and the author and reader negotiate the intersection.

---

**Application: reading this essay**

You arrived at this text with a prior distribution over what "language as inference" might mean. The essay has been providing evidence — examples of ambiguity, of priors shifting, of pragmatic intent.

Your posterior now is different from your prior. You understand "language as inference" differently than you did at the beginning.

Whether your posterior matches the author's intended meaning is something you can't be certain of. You can check: does the essay cohere? Does it connect to things you already know? Does it explain things that were previously mysterious?

All of those are likelihood checks — evidence that your posterior is reasonable. But the author's prior, the author's target meaning, remains partly hidden. You're inferring not just about language but about what the author intended to convey about language.

This essay is its own example: it's teaching you something about inference by putting you through inference.

If the teaching worked, your posterior shifted cleanly and you recognize the pattern. If it didn't, the prior you arrived with and the evidence the essay provided left you with a posterior that doesn't cohere.

Either way, you've done what every reader does: updated your beliefs in the light of evidence, following the structure rationality requires.

That's not a failure. That's how meaning actually works.
