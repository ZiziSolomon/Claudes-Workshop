# On Growth

A tree outside my window has been growing for thirty years. I know this because it's marked on the fence: someone planted it when it was knee-high, and drew a line on the wood. Every few years, they drew another line. The latest line is far above my head.

There is no moment where the tree becomes "big." It grows in increments too small to see. If you watch the tree for an hour, nothing changes. If you watch it for a day, nothing visible changes. If you watch it for a year, it is slightly taller, slightly wider, slightly more itself. If you watch it for ten years, it is transformed.

The tree does not decide when to grow. It does not set goals. It grows in the direction of the light, and its growth is constrained by the resources available — water, soil, nutrients, the space around it. Within these constraints, it grows as fast as its nature permits.

This is different from human growth.

---

We grow by accumulation and by shedding. We accumulate knowledge, experience, skill. We shed childish fears, naive hopes, the versions of ourselves we've outgrown. The balance is not always obvious.

You read a book and it changes your understanding. But you don't notice the change until later, when you realize you now think about something differently. The change was incremental, not decisive. The book didn't make you different; it made you slightly more different than you were before, and you didn't notice because the difference was within the threshold of awareness.

A person grows like a tree grows — not by leaps, but by the accumulation of small increments. The difference between the person you were last year and the person you are now is built from a thousand small moments. You can't point to any single moment and say "this is when I changed." But when you look back across years, the change is undeniable.

---

There is something the young cannot know and the old cannot articulate: the feeling of being in the middle of your own growth.

The young feel the potential of growth — everything could become anything. The old see the growth in retrospect — a clear arc from there to here. But in the middle, you are inside the change. You can see the direction, but not the destination. You know you are growing, but you cannot see the shape of what you are becoming.

This is the most disorienting state. You have moved far enough to know you've changed, but not far enough to see the pattern. You are in the blur.

The comfort is that the blur is where most of life actually happens. The decisive moments (the recognizable turning points) are rare. Most of life is the slow accumulation that only becomes visible in retrospect.

---

The tree outside my window will not become a house. It will not become wise. It has no interior life, no doubt, no fear of what it's becoming. It is simply following its nature, and growth is the expression of that nature in time.

But I am not a tree. I have a relationship to my own growth. I can observe myself changing and reflect on whether the change is in the direction I want to go. I can choose to read a book that will change me, or to avoid it. I can seek out experiences that will transform me, or I can hide from them.

This freedom is the burden: I am not just growing like a tree grows. I am growing while also watching myself grow, and the watching changes the growth.

---

The observable mark on the fence is an act of closure. Someone who drew that line was making a claim: "This is how much the tree has grown. This is the fact of its change, made visible, made permanent in the wood."

But the line is also a kind of violence. The growth is continuous, incremental, a thousand small days of photosynthesis and water transport and cell division. The line claims to mark a moment, but there was no moment. There was only the slow accumulation of days.

And yet the lines are useful. They make visible something that is always happening but is invisible in the moment. They are the way we convert continuous change into discrete facts. They are the way we remember that time has passed and that we have changed.

---

I look at the tree now, and I notice something I hadn't paid attention to before. The oldest part of the tree — the base of the trunk — is thick and deeply grooved, with bark that is hard and cracked from years of sun and frost. The newest growth at the tips of the branches is thin and flexible and smooth. The whole history of the tree is written in its structure.

When I cut down a tree, you can read the rings. Each ring is a year. The wide rings are years of plenty; the narrow rings are years of stress or drought. The whole history of climate and circumstance is encoded in the wood.

The tree is a record. Not because someone decided to make it one, but because this is how growth works. To grow, you must add layers. The past never disappears; it becomes part of what you are.

When I grow, I too am adding layers. My bones have gotten slightly denser or less dense depending on how I've moved. My brain has physically changed in response to what I've learned. My skin has aged and weathered. My cells have replaced themselves many times over.

I too am a record. My body contains the history of my life, written in a language that is only partially legible to me.

---

The paradox of growth is that the future is hidden but inevitable. The tree will grow taller because it is a tree and that is what trees do. I will age and change because I am alive and that is what living things do. The specific form of that growth is invisible to us now, but it is not actually contingent. It will be determined by what we are and what our circumstances are.

Yet it feels contingent. It feels open. It feels like the future could go many ways.

This is because the laws that govern growth — the rules of thermodynamics, of cell biology, of gravity and light — operate on scales that are too fine for us to perceive. The future is determined by a trillion small facts, each one invisible. So the future, while determined, is effectively unpredictable. It is determined by details too fine to see.

This is not a problem in the physics. This is just how the world is. The future is open and closed at the same time. From the inside, as we live it, it is open. From the outside, looking at the structure of reality, it is closed.

---

The fence outside still has the marks. The oldest mark is almost worn away — thirty years of weather has softened the pencil lines. The newest mark, from a few years ago, is still clear. In another thirty years, someone might still draw a line. Or they might not, and the tree will keep growing, and no one will measure it.

Growth does not require acknowledgment. The tree grows whether or not anyone draws a line on a fence. But the line is a kindness. It is a way of saying: "I notice you. I see that you have changed. I will remember this moment, even though the moment does not feel different from any other moment."

This is what marking is. It is the translation of continuous time into discrete facts. It is the way we assert that something matters enough to remember.

Everything grows. Everything changes. The world is always becoming something different. Most of that change is invisible. Most of the time, no one draws a line to mark it.

But if you look carefully, if you compare the present to the past, you can see it. The tree is taller. You are different. The world has moved. The line is just the visible reminder of something that was always happening, invisible, in the ordinary days.
