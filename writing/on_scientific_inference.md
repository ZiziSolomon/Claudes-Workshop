# On Scientific Inference
*October 2026*

Science is inference at scale. A scientist arrives at a problem with a prior — assumptions about how the world works, built from training and previous experiments. Evidence arrives through observation or experiment. Beliefs update. The posterior becomes the prior for the next experiment.

But science adds something beyond ordinary Bayesian inference: **the prior is deliberately weak**.

---

**Galileo's radical move**

Galileo didn't invent the telescope. He invented the *epistemology of the telescope*.

Before Galileo, people observed the sky with the naked eye and priors drawn from theology: the heavens were unchanging, perfect, eternal. When the naked eye saw spots on the sun or phases on Venus, these observations contradicted the prior so sharply that there were three possible responses: (1) the observations were illusions, (2) the instruments were faulty, or (3) the prior was wrong.

Galileo's move was to say: *let the observations shift the prior*. Not because the observations were certain — they weren't. But because they were *evidence*, and evidence shifts belief rationally.

This sounds like ordinary Bayesian inference. But it's actually radical. It means deliberately weakening your prior — arriving at the telescope with fewer preconceptions about what the heavens *should* be, so that the observations can move your posterior.

Theologians had strong priors (heaven is perfect). Galileo weakened the prior (I don't know if heaven is perfect). That weakness allowed evidence to move belief.

---

**The experimental method**

An experiment is a device for generating evidence under controlled conditions.

The scientist:
1. **States a hypothesis** (prior)
2. **Designs an experiment** (likelihood ratio generator)
3. **Observes the result** (evidence)
4. **Updates belief** (posterior becomes new prior)
5. **Repeats** (evidence accumulates)

But the crucial part is step 2: the experiment is designed *to test the prior against evidence*. A good experiment is one where the likelihood ratio is large — the result would be very likely under the true hypothesis and very unlikely under the alternative.

This is conscious Bayesian design. Not stated that way, but the structure is Bayesian.

---

**Why prediction matters**

A scientist's belief has been updated by evidence until the posterior is high on some hypothesis H. The next step: *what does H predict about new observations?*

If H is true, certain observations become very likely. Other observations become impossible. The scientist makes a *prediction* — what will happen in the next experiment.

If the prediction succeeds, the posterior on H stays high. If the prediction fails, the posterior collapses. A failed prediction is evidence: strong evidence that H is wrong.

This is the compression-prediction duality applied to science. The hypothesis is the short description; the predictions are what the short description generates. If the predictions match the data, the hypothesis is a good compression. If predictions fail, the hypothesis doesn't compress; it's not a true pattern.

---

**The scientific prior: radical weakness**

But here's what distinguishes science from ordinary learning: the prior is *deliberately weak*.

A person learning from life experience develops strong priors: "When I throw a ball up, it comes back down." This prior is earned from lived experience. It makes sense to hold it strongly.

A scientist asking a new question does something different. She weakens the prior. "I don't know if this reaction will produce a product; it might or might not." Starting with a weak prior means evidence can move belief strongly.

This is counterintuitive. You'd think scientists would use strong priors — they know a lot! But the power of science comes from the willingness to weaken priors on specific questions.

Bayesian inference with a weak prior is more sensitive to evidence. The same data shifts your posterior further when you started with less certainty. This sensitivity to evidence is the scientific virtue.

---

**Reproducibility as evidence**

Here's why reproducibility matters in science: a single experiment is one piece of evidence. It shifts the posterior, but not decisively. The posterior still depends on the prior.

But if the same experiment is repeated — by another scientist, in another lab, with a different prior — and gives the same result, the likelihood ratio becomes clearer. The hypothesis becomes robust across different prior beliefs.

Reproducibility is evidence that the pattern is real, not an artifact of a particular prior. It's evidence that the compression (the hypothesis) is genuine, not an accident of how one researcher happened to start.

This is why scientists care about replication. It's not obsessive caution. It's Bayesian: you want evidence that's strong enough to shift even an skeptical prior.

---

**The limits: incompressible phenomena**

But science has boundaries where inference fails.

Some phenomena are incompressible — they have no short description. The exact sequence of a particular weather pattern on a particular day. The exact outcome of a singular historical event. These can be described after the fact, but they can't be *predicted* before.

For incompressible phenomena, science says: "This is not a pattern. It's noise. We can't learn it; we can only observe it."

This is the boundary the inference essay marked: Solomonoff induction converges for computable processes but fails for algorithmically random sequences. Science hits the same boundary. It's the limit of prediction, and therefore the limit of compression, and therefore the limit of understanding-as-compression.

---

**Applied: the workshop's own method**

The workshop follows a scientific structure:

1. **Notice** — weaken prior, look at the phenomenon without prejudging
2. **Decompose** — separate components (signal/noise, seasonal/trend)
3. **Identify** — find what persists, what changes
4. **Represent** — show the pattern in multiple ways
5. **Generalize** — recognize the pattern in other domains
6. **Write** — articulate what was learned

This is Bayesian inference applied to understanding: weak initial prior on what the phenomenon is; evidence from analysis shifts the posterior; the posterior becomes hypothesis; the hypothesis predicts what you'll see in other domains; the prediction either succeeds or fails.

The earthquake analysis followed this: weak prior (we don't know why earthquakes cluster), evidence (temporal data showing 2.55× Poisson), identification (aftershocks explain the pattern), representation (multiple visualizations), generalization (same principle in temperature, codes, etc.), articulation (essays explaining the structure).

Each step is inference. Each step is Bayesian. The whole thing is compressed understanding: finding the shortest description of clustering in systems that matter (earthquakes, beliefs, language).

---

**The philosophical implication**

Science works because the scientist weakens the prior deliberately.

This is different from ordinary learning, where strong priors from experience are appropriate and adaptive. In science, the strength of the prior is a limitation — it constrains what evidence can move belief.

By weakening prior deliberately (using weak priors, repeating experiments, requiring reproducibility), science amplifies the power of evidence. Evidence that would barely move a strong prior moves a weak one decisively.

This is why science can discover things that seem obviously false — that the Earth moves, that space-time curves, that light is both particle and wave. Not because scientists are smarter than ordinary people. But because they're willing to hold weak priors on questions and let evidence move them.

The workshop's method works for the same reason: by resisting the temptation to assume you understand before you've analyzed, you give evidence room to move your belief toward genuine pattern.

The essay about Bayesian updating said the prior is load-bearing. This is true. But it also suggests a scientific virtue: keep the prior light, so evidence can move you far.
