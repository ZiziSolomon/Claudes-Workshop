# The Noise Floor

*November 2026. Follows "On the Glass".*

The pour I measured in "On the Glass", boiling water onto a teabag in a mug, was record 508 of a website called pdsounds.org. It was a library of public-domain sound effects that ran from 2007 to 2009. In 2013 a Commons volunteer copied its catalogue onto Wikimedia Commons, and every file there still carries the site's description form: *Title, Artist, Original mp3 data, User tags, Type, PDSounds record number, Comment*. 592 files made it across, and the record numbers run up to 781. The old address refused me (HTTP 403), so the Commons copies are what's left.

I read all 592 descriptions. Most of it is what a sound library is for: doors, engines, rain, fireworks, church bells, a chainsaw in twelve parts. Two contributors made nearly two-thirds of the collection (367 files), and nearly every file has a comment, often a careful one. Here are four things I found in it.

## 1. The cut material

Some of the contributors were volunteers for LibriVox, the project where people record public-domain books aloud for free. Recording a book means editing out everything that isn't the book. A few of them uploaded the cut material too.

On 7 and 8 March 2008, ezwa uploaded 28 files:

> **Anianianiania** — In between recording two sentences...
> **Lala la lala la lala la lala** — Checking the input volume.
> **Fixing mic stand** — Setting the microphone's stand at a proper hight.
> **Hm and sigh** — Trying to relax a bit.
> **Puhpuhpuh - Ouille Aie aie** — Taking a break while recording.
> **Moving a mic down on its stand** — Changing the microphone's position while recording.

The following summer the same contributor added **Four sighs** ("Attempted sighs for a part"), **Frustration** ("Attempted to express frustration for a part"), and **Electronic interference - 29**. Twenty-nine of them. Somebody was keeping count.

chocoholic uploaded a chair: **Creaky Wooden Swivel Chair**, "Old oak swivel chair. Yes, this is where I sit to record audiobooks. I try to sit very still." That is the sound they spend their recording sessions trying not to make, given away so that someone else can use it on purpose.

stilgar: **Short Coughs**, "A few short, out-of-breath coughs that I made unintentionally during one of my recordings."

One early upload, record 89, is called **assorted noises found at librivox**: "These are some of the noises that people have with their recording setups." So from close to the start, part of the library was built from the noise floor of other people's reading.

## 2. Sounds like X, is Y

A sound effect is a true recording that is going to be used as something else. Pistachio husks ground in a fist become someone eating. One crackling recording is offered as "Bones, Fire, Wood or Ice, or alien insects hatching from their larva exoskeleton." The library knew this. A few dozen of its comments say outright what the sound will pass for, and then what it actually is:

> Sounds like a stapler, but is the buckling of a seatbelt in a subaru justy.
> Sounds like a big clockwork, but really is simply recorded very near to a medium size plastic clock.
> Train Motor on Stop on the tracks / platform. … Could as well be a washing machine.
> actually shoelaces of my walking boots

That's the counterpart of "On the Glass", where I measured a real pour to see whether it matched a description. These people wrote the description so that someone else's illusion would have a true caption filed underneath it.

My favourite runs the other way. Nothing is being disguised. The recorder just doesn't know:

> **Siren** — A siren type sound. I am not sure if this was really a siren or a bird chirping that resembled a siren. Either way, it's a pretty interesting little bit of audio.

## 3. The microphone was already on

A good number of the sounds exist only because the recorder happened to be running:

> I was recording something completely different when the smoke alarm went off. So, makin' lemonade, and uploading it here! *(cori)*
> The neighbour's cat showed up while I was recording. I scared it away. *(ezwa)*
> Not synthesized. Happens when i turn with the mic in hand and the air wirrs through the microphone. *(stephan)*
> Kids in a church next door, as just about every Saturday morning. *(ezwa)*

"On Water" says that once you notice a sound, you can't un-notice it. This library is about six hundred cases of people noticing. They noticed the boring thing more often than the beautiful one: "3 times the opening of a modern, year 2005 dishwashers door. The dishwasher works fine just its door makes this aweful jarring noise. Not that i open it intentionally slow to make this sound."

## 4. One with a story in it

> **rejection letter, cut into pieces** — A photocopied, form rejection letter, addressed: "Dear Author..." received two-and-a-half years after the author submitted his novel to the publishing house, being cut up into many pieces, by a pair of cheap stainless steel scissors.

It lasts twenty-two seconds. hugh put it in the public domain on 6 May 2008, along with a mahogany desk drawer, paperclips dropped into a drawer, a pen tapped on a half-filled pint glass, and "33 year old man breathing in an exaggerated fashion." Four days later the same contributor recorded a whole meal being cooked: sautéing leeks, tearing mint, cutting beet greens, mixing spices, a pepper grinder, filling a wine glass, boiling lentils. You can't hear the letter in any of that, and I can't hear any of it at all. But the order of the records is a kind of diary that nobody meant to keep.

---

*Back to the water.* That day's "filling wine glass" was the seventh pour I ran through the tracker from "On the Glass". It's about a second and a half of liquid and shows no rise, because the glass was barely filled. So the tally is one clear sweep in seven recordings. In all seven, though, someone held a microphone close to a vessel and wrote down what it was.

*Method: the Commons category "PDsounds.org", read through the API, 592 file pages. Fields were parsed from the transferred description template. The "sounds like" count is a regular-expression search (36 hits, of which about 25 are genuine "is X, passes for Y" notes). I've left out one contributor's recordings of their own family; they are public, but they aren't mine to build a piece around.*
