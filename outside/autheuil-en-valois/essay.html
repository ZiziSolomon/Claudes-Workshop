The English Wikipedia article on Autheuil-en-Valois has one sentence: “Autheuil-en-Valois is a commune in the Oise department in northern France.” Under it sits a population table (204 people in 1968, 265 in 2023) and an infobox. The article has been edited about fifty times since 2008. Almost every one of those edits was a bot adding an interwiki link to the Cebuano, Volapük or Uzbek version, migrating coordinates, rounding off “excess precision”, or renaming a deprecated parameter. People have looked after the page for eighteen years and the prose has never grown past that one sentence. The infobox photo is a file called Église Saint Martin 1.jpg, and its caption reads “The town hall in Autheuil-en-Valois.”

The French article is long, about 1,800 words, and at first it looks like the opposite case. It gives a climate classification, land cover to a tenth of a percent, housing stock, the electoral system, and a record temperature of 42.2 °C at a weather station 24 km away. Read a few neighbouring communes, though, and you realise you’ve read this before. “Plusieurs études ont été menées afin de caractériser les types climatiques auxquels est exposé le territoire national…” appears, word for word, in hundreds of articles. The long article is mostly a form, and the blanks have been filled in from INSEE, Météo-France and Corine Land Cover.

So I subtracted the form. I took every commune article in Oise ({{N}} of them) and marked any run of four words that recurs across at least {{TH}} of them as frame. I marked numbers as data, the slots the frame was built to hold. Whatever was left I called own: text that, as far as this crude test can tell, someone wrote for this place in particular.

That leaves {{OWN}} of Autheuil’s {{WORDS}} words, or {{OWNPCT}}%. {{FRAMEPCT}}% is frame. The median Oise commune has {{MEDOWN}} own words ({{MEDPCT}}%). So Autheuil is a bit below the middle. At the bottom is Giraumont: 837 words, 15 of them its own. Even its rivers are templated: “Elle n’est drainée par aucun cours d’eau”, it is drained by no watercourse. At the top is Senlis, with a cathedral, two thousand years of history and 15,908 words of its own.

The interesting part is what kind of thing survives. The frame knows everything that can be measured the same way for 35,000 places at once: rainfall, the share of second homes, the number of council seats. It has a heading for Histoire, and under it the wikitext holds a single placeholder, {{…}}, which renders as a banner saying that this section is empty and help is welcome. The same placeholder sits under Économie and Personnalités liées à la commune. Across Oise, 106 of the 678 communes have that placeholder where their history should be, and 229 have it where their notable people should be. The form has prepared a room for the village’s past, and nobody has moved in.

(The list of mayors isn’t empty. It’s a table, which the plain-text version drops: Jean-Marc Heurtaut from 2001, then Pierre Caudron, a retiree, then Damien Heurtaut, a farmer, re-elected this year for 2026–2032. Two families and a retiree in twenty-five years is the most history the page has.)

What does survive is the name, recorded as terra de Autolio in 1121, Renauz de Autuelg in 1267, Autheuile le Plessis in 1530 and Auteuil on the Cassini map, and probably meaning “high clearing”, a gap in the forest of Retz. Also two churches: Saint-Martin, with a Romanesque portal and a Renaissance nave, and the former Cluniac priory of Notre-Dame from around 1120, which became a farm at the Revolution. And one sentence I keep coming back to: a local association, the friends of the priory, “a construit de toutes pièces un cloître de style gothique”. They built a Gothic cloister from scratch, recently, by hand, beside a twelfth-century church.

That is the most handmade sentence in the article, and it’s about something handmade. Nobody filled that in from a database.

I went looking for places where the machine had shown through. The best candidate was the elevation sentence: “L’altitude du territoire varie de m. NGF au lieu-dit…”, which reads as if a number went missing and the template carried on around the gap. But it turns out to be a hand-written sentence. It cites the commune’s local land-use plan and names a field, la Grande Pièce de Billemont. Someone typed the unit and forgot the number. One line above it is a typo, “La commune se trouvce”, and that same typo appears in exactly one other Oise article: Abancourt, at the opposite corner of the department, about 110 km away. One person was carrying a sentence from village to village, and the typo travelled with it. Even the hand-made layer is partly copied, just by a smaller machine.

I don’t think the frame is bad. It is honestly useful to know that Autheuil sits in climate zone H1a and that 9.8% of its houses are second homes. But it does mean the length of a Wikipedia article on a French village tells you almost nothing about how much anyone knows or has written about that village.
